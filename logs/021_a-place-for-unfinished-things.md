# Log 021: A Place for Unfinished Things

**Status:** Draft — exploratory design journal\
**Date:** 2026-09-25\
**Scope:** Making a growing notebook inhabitable without requiring every page to become a research claim.

There is a peculiar kind of silence in a well-maintained notebook. The links work. The definitions are consistent. Every unfinished sentence has been repaired. You open it to write something strange and discover that all the available forms already know what you are allowed to mean.

That is the design problem I want to work on here.

This notebook has acquired a world: machines, cities, recurring questions, fictional inhabitants, experiments that answer back. Its navigation has mostly acquired folders. A reader arrives with curiosity and is asked to select a document type.

## Start where something happens

The entrance should give the reader something to follow before it asks for a choice. A sound, a flock, a decision, someone returning to a room. One passage can lead into the next because the question changes as we read.

This has a practical consequence. A page called “Start Here” should carry a thought for a few minutes. It should not spend those minutes explaining all the revisions that made the thought publishable. The revisions need a durable home, and a reader examining the claim needs a clear route to them. Their place in the reading sequence can be chosen.

The same applies at the other end of a page. Three links with three different reasons to follow them can be more useful than a list of everything with the same keyword. One link might deepen the question, one might challenge it, and one might change the medium entirely. Those are editorial choices, not a rule that every page must have exactly three links.

## Let a page be unfinished in public

A working log can be readable before its question is resolved. An essay can be worth sharing without becoming a scientific result. A story can have an ending while leaving its world open.

Publishing these together requires a small, visible distinction about what kind of text is being offered. It does not require a verdict on the value of every sentence. “Essay,” “fiction,” “working log,” and “measured experiment” give a reader useful expectations. The experiment still needs its methods and limitations. The story needs room for things its characters cannot explain.

The old site boundary treated public appearance as a proxy for being finished. This log proposes a different editorial choice: publish a text when someone can meaningfully encounter it, and make its condition legible. Some raw notes will still be better kept near the working files. Some old logs are already readable and should be reachable without leaving the notebook.

## Make room for a different next question

There is a temptation to turn this into another improvement procedure. Every story produces a hypothesis; every hypothesis produces an experiment; every experiment comes home to strengthen the framework. The sequence looks productive. It also gives the future very little freedom.

Sometimes an experiment should send us away from the framework. Sometimes a story should make an older question seem uninteresting. Sometimes an essay should discover that it belongs beside a piece of music and remain there.

A small web can make these possibilities ordinary. Keep the direct links. Write down why two distant pages became neighbours. Let a dated entry show that a previously central subject has gone quiet. Leave an old path walkable without pretending it is still the only entrance.

There is a limit: a reader can become lost in generosity. If every paragraph opens ten doors, the text never gets to finish a thought. A route therefore needs stretches with no decisions, and a way to return to familiar ground. Navigation is also the decision to let someone read for a while.

## Try the walk

The first walk starts with [a sound and its possible causes](../docs/synthesis.md). A second starts with [what a world can remember](../theory/narrative/what-a-world-can-remember.md). The new story, [The Weather Between Us](../fiction/20_the_weather_between_us.md), can be read on its own. None requires completing the others.

The immediate check is modest: can a new reader follow a thought, understand why a link is offered, recognize when the kind of writing changes, and find a way back? If the labels overwhelm the opening, or if the routes keep funneling every encounter into the same formal answer, this design needs another pass. There is no reader study behind it yet.

After that, the notebook needs ordinary use. Another entry. A disagreement. Something we did not know how to file when it arrived.
