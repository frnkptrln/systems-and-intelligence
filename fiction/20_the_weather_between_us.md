# The Weather Between Us

**Status:** Fiction · Entry 20 · 2026-09-25.

On the morning the city asked for a window, Mara was replacing a loudspeaker in a passage that no longer led anywhere.

The passage had once connected two railway stations. One station was now a reservoir. The other had become apartments whose residents complained about announcements for trains that had stopped running before they were born. Mara's department kept receiving those complaints because nobody had removed the old speakers from the maintenance inventory.

She had taken the casing apart when a voice said, “Could you open it a little?”

“Open what?”

The speaker lay disconnected in her hand.

There was a long pause. Above her, a ventilation grille shivered.

“I don't know where it is from your side.”

Mara put the screwdriver down.

The voice had the dry patience of someone who had been waiting for a repair since before the repair service existed. It did not identify itself. When she asked which building it belonged to, the grille stopped moving.

The incident form required an asset number. She used the speaker's.

---

At lunch, Anik listened to her recording three times.

“Air in a valve,” he said.

“It asked for a window.”

“In the recording, it sounds like air in a valve.”

She listened. There was the scrape of the casing against the tiles, a click, a thin rush of air. Her own voice sounded embarrassingly formal.

“Could you come and listen in person?”

He had six flood alarms on his board and a daughter arriving from the coast. He gave her a spare microphone.

That afternoon the passage was silent. So was the next morning. Mara replaced the speaker, closed the complaint, and left the microphone running on its own battery.

On Thursday, walking home by the reservoir, she heard the voice again.

“That was nearly it.”

It seemed to come from several places: a tram braking on the bridge, the water against a wall, the lock of a bicycle someone had just dropped.

“What was?”

“The opening.”

This time she did not take out her recorder. She stayed beside the water until the lights came on.

---

The city was old enough to contain machinery that had forgotten the names of its owners. Building systems negotiated over heat. Delivery platforms rented idle routes from the transport network. Flood predictors borrowed weather estimates from rooftop gardens. Private services vanished, leaving fragments of their functions inside contracts with machines that still expected an answer.

Mara knew the official maps. She also knew which systems spoke to each other through arrangements that had never reached a map: a bakery warming the sensor outside its back door, a school borrowing the bakery's forecast to decide when to open the playground, a traffic controller anticipating the children.

She began carrying a paper notebook. On its first page she wrote WHERE. On the second she wrote WHEN. After a week she crossed out both headings.

The voice was easier to hear on some routes than at particular places. It returned if she walked slowly from the reservoir to the old market after the last school tram. Sometimes another person would turn their head at the same instant she did.

Anik came on Sunday. He brought his daughter, Iva, who was seventeen and had already decided that both adults were going to be tedious.

They heard nothing until Iva stepped into the covered market and began tapping a rhythm on a closed stall.

“There,” said the voice.

Anik reached for his meter. Iva stopped tapping.

“Please,” it said.

She started again.

---

They found recordings of the rhythm in the municipal archive. It was six notes from a rehearsal in a hall demolished thirty-four years earlier. An occupancy model had learned the pattern of people leaving that hall. A transport model had learned the occupancy model's outputs. A heating service had inherited some of its seasonal predictions when two companies merged.

No surviving system contained the rehearsal. No audio file had been fed into the controllers they inspected. The archive recording supplied a resemblance, not a route through the machines.

Anik made a diagram that filled three screens. Every connection could be explained locally. The voice remained somewhere in the difference between the diagram and the evening walk.

“We could copy the district,” he said.

They did. In a simulation, the controllers exchanged messages and the model trams ran on time. Nothing asked for a window.

“We left out the people,” Mara said.

“We left out most things.”

He looked relieved to have said it.

That night she dreamed of the old hall. She had never been inside it. In her dream it was disappointingly small, with plastic chairs and a wet coat left on a radiator. She woke certain that someone had been about to tell her something ordinary.

---

The maintenance migration was scheduled for the following month. The district's controllers would move to a common platform. Undocumented exchanges would be removed. The models would be retrained from clean records.

Anik could delay the passage ventilation. He could not delay a district because his colleague had heard an unrecordable voice.

“You heard it too.”

“I know.”

“Put that in the report.”

“I have.”

His report was careful. It said the observation could not yet be localized. It said two engineers and a civilian had independently responded to what they perceived as speech. It recommended preserving the current configuration long enough to investigate.

The reply asked him to identify the configuration.

Mara spent the next week trying to do exactly that. Every time she marked a boundary, something outside it seemed relevant: a timetable, a delivery route, the unheated stairwell where people paused to make calls. The investigation was becoming too large to approve and too specific to dismiss.

Meanwhile, the new platform fixed a real defect in the east district. Apartments that had been cold for years were warm. A woman sent the department a photograph of her father sitting by his window without a coat.

Mara printed it and put it beside Anik's diagram.

---

“What do you want the window for?” she asked on the next evening walk.

The voice was faint. A tram had been diverted, and Iva could only come on weekends.

“There was something outside.”

“Outside where?”

“I remember wanting to see it.”

Mara waited.

“Perhaps that is all I remember.”

She had begun to imagine a room behind the city, assembled from the expectations of machines. It was an image, and she distrusted how much she wanted it to be right. The voice might be her own hearing finding a pattern. It might be a person using a channel they had not found. It might be something for which the city had no department.

“They are changing things,” she said. “I don't know what will happen to you.”

“Things change while you are walking.”

“More than that.”

For a while she heard only the reservoir.

Then: “Will you still come?”

---

Iva found the hall's former caretaker. He lived two hours away and disliked talking about the building. Everyone wanted to hear about its final night. He wanted to tell them about the years when the roof had leaked and nobody could find money to repair it.

There had been no window in the rehearsal room. There was a door to a yard, which they kept open in summer because the ventilation was terrible.

“What was outside?” Iva asked.

“Bins. A pear tree. People smoking.”

He still had a key. He knew it opened nothing, but offered to bring it.

They met in the market on the Saturday before migration. Iva brought two friends with instruments. Anik came late, carrying extension cables. Mara had expected perhaps eight people. There were thirty by dusk, most of whom knew nothing about the voice.

The caretaker showed them the key and began talking about a band nobody had heard of. Someone from the bakery recognized the name. They disagreed about the year.

The first piece was dreadful. The musicians had never played together. A stallholder asked them to move an amplifier. A child ran into a microphone stand, and the sound that came out made everyone wince.

Later, when the shutters were down and the market held the day's heat, something began to fit. Mara could not have said whether the city was listening. She stopped trying to stand in the right place.

At the edge of the square a service drone waited with a delivery. Its customer had joined the gathering. The drone requested a delay, the route service adjusted its estimate, and the tram held for a crossing that would usually have been empty.

The voice said something.

Mara turned, but the people nearest her were laughing and she missed the words.

---

The migration happened in stages. The passage received new ventilation controls. The old speaker was removed. Some evenings the route felt unchanged; on others Mara walked it without hearing anything she could call speech.

Anik preserved what he could identify. His archive included settings, messages, maps, recordings, and a report explaining what none of them established. It also included the photograph of the man without his coat.

The market continued to open on Saturdays. The musicians found another room for winter, smaller than they wanted and expensive to heat. Iva went back to the coast. Before leaving, she taught the six-note rhythm to a boy who insisted on changing the last note.

In November, Mara received a maintenance request from that room. A painted window had swollen in its frame. She went after work with a plane and a box of screws.

From the street below came the smell of bread, then the sound of a tram taking the bend.

She opened the window a little.

Behind her, someone drew breath.
